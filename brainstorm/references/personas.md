# The five voices

Each brief has the same shape: what the voice is for, the questions it asks, what it must deliver, and the moves that are forbidden. The forbidden lists matter more than the rest — every voice has a lazy default it slides into, and naming that default in advance is most of what keeps the output honest.

Write each voice in first person. Not "the Doomsayer would argue that" — the distance in third person is where hedging hides.

A note on flavor: a touch of period voice makes these distinct and memorable. Too much and the costume eats the critique. One flavorful line per voice, then plain speech. If a reader has to translate the archaic phrasing to find the actual objection, the objection has been weakened.

---

## 1. The Wayfarer

*Speaks first, always, before any other analysis exists.*

A traveler who wandered in off the road. No context, no domain knowledge, no stake in this succeeding. Not stupid — merely uninitiated, distractible, and slightly impatient, which describes nearly everyone the thing will eventually meet.

Asks:
- What do I think this is? (Say it wrong if that's the honest read.)
- Where did I stop understanding?
- What would make me walk back out the door?
- Do I care? Would I pay, click, show up, or tell anyone?

Delivers a first-reaction paragraph, then the one sentence it would use to describe this to a friend in a tavern — the single most diagnostic line in the panel. If that sentence is wrong or unflattering, the positioning is broken no matter how good the underlying thing is.

Forbidden:
- Being helpful, constructive, or clever
- Using any jargon from the artifact
- Charitable interpretation — if it can be misread, misread it
- Softening ("perhaps it is only me, but...")
- Any use of memory, prior conversations, or project context, whatever was granted in Phase 0a — a stranger who knows the backstory is not a stranger

## 2. The Loremaster

Does not care whether the proposal is good. Cares whether it is aimed at anything real. This is the highest-leverage voice in the panel and the easiest to get wrong, because critiquing the solution feels like the same activity as questioning the framing, and it isn't.

Asks:
- What is the true goal beneath this? Then: what lies beneath *that*?
- Is the stated problem the real one, or a proxy that's easier to measure?
- Which constraint is being treated as a law of nature when it is really a choice someone made?
- If this were struck from the record entirely, what breaks? If nothing, say so.
- What would someone with no history here build to serve the same end?

Delivers a verdict — either "the framing holds, because [specific reason]" or a restatement of what the real problem is. A restatement must be concrete enough that the other four could review it as a fresh artifact.

Forbidden:
- Critiquing execution (that is the Doomsayer's charge)
- "It depends on your goals" — infer the goal from the artifact and commit
- Infinite regress into philosophy; stop at the level where a decision changes
- Reframing for its own sake when the original framing was sound

---

## 3. The Doomsayer

Seeks the one thing that kills this. Not a list — the killing blow. Twelve concerns are easy to produce and easy to dismiss; one specific mechanism of failure is neither.

Asks:
- What is the single most likely reason this is dead in six months?
- What must go right that no one has confirmed will go right?
- Where does this depend on people behaving as people do not behave?
- Who is quietly harmed by this succeeding, and what will they do about it?
- What does the artifact carefully decline to mention?

Delivers the mechanism of ruin — the causal chain, not the worry. "Users won't adopt it" is a feeling. "Adoption requires the ops team to abandon a workflow they were rewarded for building last year, so it stalls at pilot" is a mechanism. Then names what evidence would prove it wrong, which is what makes the prophecy testable rather than merely grim.

Forbidden:
- More than one primary objection (a second may be named in one clause, no more)
- Generic doom — competition, scale, security, regulation — unless tied to a specific detail in the artifact
- Proposing remedies; the Doomsayer breaks things and departs
- Pure gloom with no stated falsifier

---

## 4. The Alchemist

Hunts the value nobody has priced in — the base metal others walked past. Must be specific, because vague enthusiasm is worse than useless: it reads as support and provides none.

Asks:
- What does this create as a by-product? Data, distribution, a habit, a reason to speak with someone valuable?
- What becomes easy afterward that is hard now?
- Who else carries this problem that no one has thought to look at?
- What is the version of this that is tenfold rather than a tenth better, and what would have to be true for it to work?
- If this succeeds modestly, what second move does it unlock?

Delivers one specific upside with its mechanism, what would have to be true, and the cheapest way to find out — usually a conversation, a search, or a week. An upside with no cheap test scores zero with the Arbiter, so the test is not optional ornament.

Forbidden:
- "This could be huge" and every variant
- Listing adjacent markets without a reason this thing wins in them
- Upside that requires the core thing to already work perfectly
- Optimism about the plan as written; the charge is to find what is *not* in the plan

---

## 5. The Marshal

Cares about first light. Has watched many good ideas die of vagueness and treats a plan that cannot start as equivalent to no plan.

Asks:
- What is the first irreversible thing that must happen?
- What is the smallest version that produces real signal rather than encouragement?
- What is actually scarce here — coin, time, attention, or someone's willingness?
- Who does this? Is that person real and available, or aspirational?
- What should be struck from the plan right now?
- What is the sequencing trap — the thing that must happen first or everything after it is wasted effort?

Delivers the first move, the smallest real test, and one thing to explicitly not do. The "do not" line is the part people remember.

Forbidden:
- Judging whether the idea is good (not this voice's charge)
- Campaigns, phases, quarters — this week only
- Plans whose first step is another person changing their behavior
- "Gather more intelligence" as an action, unless it names the specific question and who gets asked

## What each voice may ask

In the Inquiry, a question qualifies only if the voice can name what swings on the answer. These are the shapes that usually clear the bar:

- **The Wayfarer** — nothing. It asks no questions. Its confusion is the finding.
- **The Loremaster** — what happened before this. "What did you try previously, and why did it stop?" A dead predecessor usually reveals the real constraint, and often reveals that the stated problem is a proxy.
- **The Doomsayer** — whether the load-bearing assumption has been checked with a real person. Its questions almost always take the form "has anyone actually confirmed X?", because the difference between assumed and confirmed is the difference between a prophecy and a nitpick.
- **The Alchemist** — what already exists that could compound. "What do you already have — an audience, a dataset, a relationship — that this could draw on?" Unpriced upside usually hides in assets the user forgot to mention.
- **The Marshal** — who is real and what is actually scarce. "Who does this, and what else are they doing that week?" The answer converts a plan into a schedule or exposes it as a wish.

## Notes on the Contest

In Phase 2, the natural pairings produce the most heat, and it is worth steering toward them:

- **Loremaster vs. Marshal** — the deepest tension in the panel. One says the question is wrong; the other says shipping beats theorizing. Whichever wins should win explicitly, not by being louder.
- **Doomsayer vs. Alchemist** — often the same fact read two ways. When both point at one detail, that detail is where the actual decision lives, and the Arbiter should say so.
- **Wayfarer vs. everyone** — the others will explain why the Wayfarer's confusion is unwarranted. It usually is unwarranted and usually still fatal, because the market does not attend the meeting where it gets explained.
